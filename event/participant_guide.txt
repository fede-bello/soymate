Hack the Fork in brief
The food system is at a turning point. Alternative proteins are among the most promising sectors of
food technology, but slow research and development is hindering their growth.

Our model rests on three pillars:

Hackathons. We bring together high-calibre students and young professionals (engineers, data
scientists, food and management experts, and business professionals) with food technology and
foodtech companies to solve real-world industrial challenges. Artificial intelligence (AI) allows us to
condense months of research into a single weekend. For our partners, it's also an opportunity to
identify talented individuals comfortable with technology.

Pre-incubation. The best prototypes don't necessarily end with hackathons. We help the most
promising teams refine them through pre-incubation support and connect them with investors.

Career mentoring. Whether you have a startup project or not, we can help you build a career in
alternative proteins. Participating in the hackathon is a gateway to career support if you wish.

Human ingenuity, AI-powered acceleration, and market-driven partnerships: this is how Hack the
Fork transforms ideas into businesses that make food sustainable. In 2026, 11 editions are taking
place in 8 countries. The Paris edition, hosted by AgroParisTech, carries out this mission with its
own ecosystem, partners, and student community.

This hackathon is not an academic exercise: the runner-up of the 2025 edition, ReaKt, is
currently incubated at Genopole and is well on its way to launching its product on the market.

Your team, and what you can do now
Size. Teams consist of 2 to 5 people. Teams that combine technical, scientific, and business
profiles have a significant advantage: the jury's evaluation criteria assess scientific rigor, AI
integration, economic viability, impact, and quality of execution. A team with a single profile
struggles in four out of five areas.

How teams are formed. A team spreadsheet has been shared with you. It contains the list of
participants, their profiles, and those still looking for teammates. This will allow you to identify
those you would like to work with and contact them before the event. Teams will meet on-site and
begin working on Friday at 8:45 PM, following the first workshop. From 5:45 PM to 6:45 PM, there
will be a networking session for participants to connect and identify potential partners.

Challenge selection. On Friday evening, each team chooses one of the three challenges and
declares it to the organizers before 11 p.m. Take the time to discuss it together and, if you are
unsure, ask a mentor for advice: a well-chosen challenge from the start will save you time.
What you can do before the hackathon starts. Read this guide and the challenge brief, research
the sector, meet your teammates, discuss the problem, install your tools, and explore the open
resources listed below. It's even recommended: a team that comes prepared with some knowledge
of the sector saves several hours compared to others.

Things you cannot do before the start of the hackathon. Build the solution, the code, the model,
the prototype, the challenge-specific results, or the final pitch deck. All deliverables must be
produced during the event, or the team will be disqualified.

Pre-existing resources must be declared. If you use code, research, datasets, designs, or
pre-trained models created before the hackathon, you must declare them when you submit your
project. Open-source licenses remain the original ones. A completed project or a patented solution
is not eligible.

Before leaving on Sunday. Retrieve and save (to a USB drive, internal/external hard drive, GitHub,
etc.) everything your team has produced using AWS tools. Note: accounts will be closed after the
event and nothing will be recoverable afterward.

At the end of the hackathon. Each participant will receive a personalized certificate of
participation, attesting to their commitment during these three days. Hack the Fork also offers
post-hackathon programs for those who wish to continue their project or build an impactful career
in food. Access to these programs will be by selection.


Mentoring
Throughout the weekend, mentors from research, industry and entrepreneurship are there to
support you: Friday evening, Saturday and Sunday morning.

Their role. They help you define your problem, test your assumptions, compare your idea with
industry realities, and refine your presentation. Rather than giving you answers, they ask the right
questions: that's what moves a project forward.

What they don't do. They don't write your code, your model, or your presentation: the project
remains entirely yours. They also don't participate in the evaluation, which allows you to
communicate with them freely, including about your doubts and difficulties.


The challenges of this edition
Before presenting the challenges, here is the context on which the framework is based. The jury is
already aware of this: any team that ignores these points will be challenged during the Q&A.

Plant-based proteins: the challenges facing the sector

  •​ Price, the primary obstacle to adoption. A plant-based product often costs more than its
     animal-based equivalent. To reduce this gap, action must be taken across the entire chain,
     from sourcing to production, and not just by improving recipes.
  •​ Institutional catering: an underutilized resource. The law sets several obligations for
     institutional catering: in school canteens, at least one vegetarian menu per week; in all
     institutional catering, at least 50% sustainable and high-quality products, including 20%
     ​organic products; and since January 2024, at least 60% sustainable and high-quality meat
     and fish in public institutional catering. Restaurants serving more than 200 meals per day
     must also establish a multi-year protein diversification plan. However, there are still few
     digital tools available to help them develop this plan.
  •​ Consumer confidence in ultra-processed products. Consumers increasingly want short and
     easy-to-read ingredient lists. The challenge is to shorten them without sacrificing texture,
     taste, or consistency.
  •​ Dependence on imports. France currently imports a large portion of the plant-based
     proteins it consumes. Legumes occupy approximately 4% of its agricultural land, and the
     goal is to double this share by 2030.
  •​ Private label brands: a growth engine with limited resources. They operate in several
     segments, such as plant-based drinks, but their research and development (R&D) budgets
     are much lower than those of major brands.

Precision fermentation: the challenges of the sector

  •​ Scale-up, the first bottleneck. Moving from laboratory to factory remains slow, costly and
     risky. To overcome this obstacle, Danone, Michelin and DMC Biotechnologies have invested
     in a dedicated platform in Clermont-Ferrand, the Biotech Open Platform (BIOP).
  •​ Few simulation tools are available. Tools such as digital twins are lacking to predict process
     performance before launching costly physical trials. Those that do exist are often reserved
     for large corporations.
  •​ Very high capital requirements. Even a start-up that has already received funding needs to
     raise tens of millions of euros to build a single factory.

Cross-cutting issues

   •​ Active but underfunded research. France ranks 7th in Europe for the number of
      publications on alternative proteins, but its public funding remains far behind that of the
      United Kingdom and the Netherlands.
   •​ The transition of agricultural and livestock sectors. Livestock farmers fear competition
      that would threaten their incomes, and this fear is hindering political support. Alternative
      proteins must be developed with farmers, not against them.

Three challenges are proposed. On Friday evening, your team chooses one and responds with a
solution where AI plays a real role. Each challenge poses a deliberately broad question: it's up to
you to narrow it down to a specific problem for a clearly identified user. Over to you!

Track 1 · Precision fermentation, from the lab to the factory
The problem. In France, research in precision fermentation exists, but industrialization is stalled.
Moving from a high-performing strain in the laboratory to profitable production remains slow,
expensive, and risky, and every large-scale error is very costly. Accessible simulation tools, such
as digital twins, are lacking to predict process performance before investing in physical trials.

The challenge. How can artificial intelligence reduce the risk and cost of scaling up precision
fermentation?

Track 2 · Plant-based foods on French plates
The problem. Plant-based products remain more expensive than their animal-based counterparts.
Institutional catering, which could significantly influence consumer habits, lacks the tools to
implement its protein diversification obligations under the EGAlim law. Meanwhile, consumers are
wary of overly long ingredient lists and processed foods.

The challenge. How can artificial intelligence make plant-based proteins cheaper, simpler, and
more present in everyday meals?

Useful open resources:

   •​ Nutritional composition table (ANSES)
   •​ Agribalyse environmental database (ADEME)
   •​ Open Food Facts (ingredient lists and compositions of commercial products)
   •​ Government platform “Ma Cantine” and guides from the National Council for Collective
      Catering (vegetarian menu, protein diversification plan)

Track 3 · Protein sovereignty, from French fields to the proteins of tomorrow
The problem. Approximately 4% of French agricultural land is dedicated to pulses, with the goal of
doubling this share by 2030. For plant-based proteins to replace a growing proportion of animal
proteins in the human diet, France needs to produce more pulses for human consumption, rather
than livestock feed. This transition is hampered by farmers' and livestock breeders' fears of
income loss in a highly competitive market.

The challenge. How can artificial intelligence help farmers and regions to produce the plant-based
proteins that the French will eat tomorrow, and to generate income from them?

Useful open resources:

   ●​    Agricultural statistics Agreste (Ministry of Agriculture)

   ●​    Terres Inovia, the technical institute for oilseeds and pulses

Important: List of topics excluded from the scope of the Hack the Fork hackathon

  •​ Insects.
  •​ Products containing ingredients of animal origin.
  •​ Animal feed. The projects are aimed at direct human consumption: developing plant-based
     proteins for livestock, including replacing imported soy, is outside the scope.
  •​ Projects without functional demonstration, which show nothing concrete.

A project that falls into these categories is not eligible, regardless of its quality.

If you're unsure, ask a mentor or the organization early. A problem reported on Friday evening
costs a few minutes; the same problem discovered on Sunday costs the whole weekend.


Best practices
Regardless of the challenge chosen, the jury looks for the same signs:

    ●​    A specific user and a decision that your solution helps them make. "Manufacturers" are
          not a user; "the purchasing manager of a central kitchen" is.
    ●​    A demonstration that works, even partial. A mockup of screens is not enough.
   ●​   An AI that can be understood. You can explain simply what it does, what data it relies on,
        and what its margin of error is. It brings real value to the solution: if we removed it, your
        project would lose most of its appeal.
   ●​   Traceable data, by distinguishing what is measured, sourced, assumed or synthetic.
   ●​   Acknowledged limits. Stating what your model cannot do is a sign of rigor.

A useful test before pitching: what would your solution lose if the AI ​component were removed? If
the answer is "not much", you have a problem with the Authentic Integration of AI dimension.


AI, data and infrastructure
Your AWS account. On Friday, October 30, each team will receive login codes for AWS tools,
along with the associated credits, provided as part of our partnership. Access will be sent to the
email address you provided on the application form, and you will use this same email address to
log in. To get started with the tools, consult the AWS webinar guide and notes (sent via email), as
well as this video. If you have any problems, contact Morgane Paris on WhatsApp (+33 6 44 95 54
05). Please note that the account will be closed after the hackathon: be sure to save all your work.

AI should carry the solution, not dress it up. A generic conversational layer is insufficient if the
same result could be achieved without it. AI must contribute to analysis, prediction, optimization,
simulation, computer vision, or evidence gathering.

Approaches that count as real-world use. Predictive modeling, recommendation or ranking,
optimization, computer vision, LLM and RAG on credible sources, simulation, segmentation. The
list is not exhaustive: what matters is that AI changes the outcome.

Data transparency. Clearly distinguish, in your pitch as well as in your submission, what is
measured or sourced, what is based on a hypothesis, and what is synthetic or produced by a
model. Synthetic data is permitted for demonstration purposes, provided it is clearly identified: it
should never be presented as experimental results.

Sources. Document the datasets, literature, models, and external tools you use. Check the
references generated by a language model before displaying them: a fabricated citation is easily
detected and negatively impacts the scientific and technical rigor dimension.

Integrity. Fabricating results, falsifying a test, plagiarizing a project, or presenting someone else's
work as your own can lead to the team's disqualification.


Pitch and evaluation grid
Format. Each team has 4 minutes for their presentation, followed by 2 minutes of questions from
the jury. This time is the same for all teams and must be strictly adhered to: at the end of the 4
minutes, the presentation stops, even if you haven't finished.

Language. The pitches can be presented in French or English, at each team's choice.

All teams follow the same structure: the problem, your solution, a live demonstration or prototype,
and then your scaling strategy. We advise you to set aside time for the demonstration: it's often
what convinces the jury.

Rules, Intellectual Property and Code of Conduct
Your intellectual property

It belongs to you 100%. The intellectual property created during the hackathon remains the
exclusive property of the team members who created it. Neither Hack the Fork, nor AgroParisTech,
nor any partners acquire any rights to your work as a result of your participation.

No automatic rights for partners. Some partners may have a right of first look: after the
hackathon, they can offer the winning teams the opportunity to discuss a license, a research
collaboration, or a partnership. This right does not transfer any ownership to them and does not
obligate you in any way: you remain free to accept or decline.

Continuing the project. You are not obligated to continue your project after the hackathon. If Hack
the Fork wishes to take over a project you abandon, any transfer or license must be voluntary,
written, and validated: nothing is transferred automatically.

Knowledge sharing. Each team provides a short, non-confidential summary of its project,
covering the problem addressed, the solution adopted, and the business model. This summary
feeds into Hack the Fork's annual report and is one of the submission deliverables.

Competition Integrity

Produced during the event. The solution, code, model, prototype specific to the challenge and the
final deck must be developed during the event.

Previous work declared. Desk research, reusable generic libraries and pre-existing resources can
be used, under the declaration conditions described above.

Fairness. All teams are subject to the same rules, deadlines, and evaluation criteria, and have the
same resources. To maintain this equality, use only the data, tools, infrastructure, and templates
authorized by the rules. If you have any doubts, contact Hack the Fork before using them.

Code of Conduct

Hack the Fork aims to be an inclusive, supportive, and collaborative space. No form of
discrimination, harassment, intimidation, or disrespectful behavior will be tolerated, whether
towards participants, mentors, jury members, volunteers, or the organizing team. Any violation
may result in exclusion from the event.

If you witness or are a victim of such a situation, report it immediately to Inès Mir-Moreaux by
email (ines.mir-moreaux@hackthefork.org) or by telephone (+33 6 77 28 65 30).

Every report will be taken seriously.
